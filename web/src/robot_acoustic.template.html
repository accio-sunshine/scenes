Scene Simulator

Hear a scene the way a robot with a ring of microphones on its head hears it. Pick a place (a home, a factory floor, a hospital room or a car), set the room, place up to five people anywhere around the robot, in 360 degrees and up or down, and add the noise that place brings with it. The robot's own fan, servos and electronics add noise right next to its microphones.

What this page is for: to hear and measure how well the robot can pick out one person's voice when other people and the environment are making noise. It simulates the robot's microphones for the scene you set up, then runs the same processing as the Python scripts: direction finding (where each sound comes from) and beamforming (combining the microphones to focus on one speaker). Use it to try ideas quickly, such as a louder machine, a closer speaker, a more echoey room or more microphones, before running the full Python simulation.

How to use it

  1. Pick a sceneHome, Factory, Hospital or Automobile loads a room, people and noise sources. Custom (classic tests) has the four original test scenes.
  2. Shape the room and the arrayUnder Scene setup, change the room size, echo and damping, and the number and layout of microphones.
  3. Place people and noiseAdd up to 5 speakers, set their voice, loudness and start time, and drag them in the top view. Switch noise sources on or off.
  4. Listen to the raw micsPress Play selected mics. Click mics on the head to choose which you hear. Use Stereo with headphones.
  5. Compare the processingUnder Pick out each speaker, play Mic 1 alone, then the beamformers aimed at each target speaker.
  6. Read the scoresThe Scores table compares each method with the original (mic 1 alone). Click any row to see its waveform and spectrogram. Download everything as a zip.

Scores

Each score is how much stronger a target voice is than everything else at that point, in dB. The original is Mic 1 alone: what one microphone hears with no processing. Gain is how much the processing improves on the original. +3 dB means the voice is about twice as strong relative to the noise, and +10 dB means ten times. Speakers switched to interferer are not scored.

      Python recordings

      The WAV files made by sim_conversation.py and sim_8mic_robot_noise.py with pyroomacoustics for the classic test scenes (Custom). Open one to compare it with the page's version.

      How the page makes the sound

      The room is a box of the size you set, with early wall reflections plus a statistical late echo. Echo (RT60) sets how long the sound lingers; Damping makes the surfaces absorb more at high frequencies. The classic scenes use the same room (6 × 5 × 3 m), 8-mic ring, voices, robot noise recipes, levels and beamformers as the Python scripts and land within about 0.5 dB of Python in the baby and fan scenes.

      Scenario noise is synthesised

      No recorded ambience is used. The television, machines, monitors and engine are generated from simple models, so how realistic a scenario sounds is a qualitative judgement. Footsteps are approximate: the page's beamformer cancels the thump much less than Python does, so footsteps start switched off.

      Loudness levels

      Every level is loudness at mic 1 compared with Speaker 1's voice, as in the scripts' LEVEL_DB and BABY_DB. The 8-mic limit applies only downstream: the PDM Frontend-in-the-Loop pipeline needs exactly 8 microphones.
